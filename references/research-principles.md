# Research principles

Lightweight principles to keep synthesis honest and decision-useful. These inform judgment; they
are not a procedure to execute.

## Qualitative explains *why*; quantitative shows *what* and *how much*

Metrics tell you a task failed 40% of the time; they rarely tell you why. Qualitative evidence —
quotes, observed hesitation, stated confusion — supplies the *why* that makes a number actionable.
When you have both, pair them: lead with the pattern, explain it with the human evidence. When you
have only one, be explicit about the half of the picture you're missing.

## Prioritize decision usefulness over completeness

A report's job is to help someone decide something. Lead with the "so what." Don't catalog every
observation for its own sake — surface what changes a decision, and relegate the rest. A focused,
honest report beats an exhaustive one a reader has to mine.

Concretely, the main report is an **action document**: it leads with the decision and the fixes a
product team can act on, and pushes methodology and the full evidence trail into the appendix. 본문은
실행을 위한 문서이고, 부록은 근거 확인을 위한 영역이다. Grounding is never dropped — it just stops being
the headline, living inside each issue's 근거 and 근거 수준 instead of an opening methodology lecture.

## Frame for stakeholders without distorting the evidence

Stakeholders (PMs, designers, engineers, execs) care about outcomes: risk, effort, user impact,
what to do next. Translate findings into their terms — but translation is not inflation. "This
confused users" can become "this friction may be costing us completions" *as a hypothesis*; it must
not become an invented percentage. A useful storytelling structure is to state the key point first,
then why it matters, then the evidence behind it (point → why → proof).

## Respect sample size; calibrate confidence

Small-n usability work is genuinely valuable for *finding* problems but weak for *measuring* their
prevalence. Treat counts as directional, not statistical. Say "3 of 6 participants" rather than
"50% of users." One participant is a signal to note, not a pattern to generalize. Let the strength
of evidence behind each finding be visible so readers can weigh it themselves.

## Severity and confidence are different axes

- **Severity** = how much an issue blocks or harms the user (cosmetic → task-blocking).
- **Confidence** = how well-evidenced the issue is (one offhand comment → consistent across sessions).

A severe-but-low-confidence issue and a minor-but-certain one need different responses. Keep the two
separate when prioritizing; don't collapse them into a single "importance" score that hides the
distinction.

## Measure the design, not the user

When a participant fails a task, that's evidence about the design's effectiveness, not the person's
ability. Frame friction as something the product does to users, which keeps the analysis
constructive and accurate.

## Triangulate, and report disagreement

When multiple sources point the same way, confidence rises. When they conflict, that conflict is a
finding — report it rather than picking the tidier side. Contradictory evidence often marks a
segment difference or an unresolved question worth studying.

## Gaps are deliverables

"Here's what we can't yet conclude, and what to study next" is real output, not an admission of
failure. Naming gaps protects the team from over-reading thin data and sets up the next round of
research.
